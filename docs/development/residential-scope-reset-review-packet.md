# Residential Building Science Scope Review Packet

_Phase 1 draft for the course developer to review. The proposed wording and artifact changes below have not been adopted as final curriculum._

## Course Subject and Classroom Setting

The course developer clarified that the subject is the building science of an ordinary single-family home. Seventh- and eighth-grade students will study that subject in a school classroom. The current [course concept](../../middle-school-building-science-course-concept.md) describes buildings broadly and suggests observing the school building; it does not yet state the residential focus. Several [draft prototypes](prototypes/) consequently use learning studios, classrooms, a library, or school facility systems as their main building.

The confirmed delivery constraints remain nine weeks, three 40-minute meetings per week, approximately 15 students, a standard classroom with a teacher display and student laptops, and no more than one assumed field trip. Heat flow remains approved for development as a recurring explanatory idea. Course outcomes, essential strands, the organizing question, assessment, and the 27-meeting map still need the developer's decisions.

## Proposed Wording for the Course Concept

The following text is offered for review before changing the course concept.

### Purpose

> This course introduces seventh- and eighth-grade students to the building science of an ordinary single-family home. A home is a system whose enclosure, equipment, occupants, and surrounding conditions interact. Students use models and supplied evidence to explain how heat, air, and water can move through a home and how those interactions can affect comfort, indoor conditions, durability, and energy use. These are examples of possible course content; the final content strands remain to be selected.

### Central Course Idea

> A home is more than a box with four walls. Its parts interact with one another, the people living in it, and the environment around it.

### Learning Experience

> The course takes place in a standard classroom. Students can investigate general physical processes with simple classroom materials, then apply their reasoning to diagrams, photographs with reuse rights, and supplied fictional or approved examples of single-family homes. Participation does not depend on visiting, measuring, photographing, or describing a student's home. School-building observations may illustrate a general process when useful, but the home remains the main case.

The proposed wording identifies the building type without choosing a particular home design, climate, age, technology package, or final strand sequence. It also allows students with different housing experiences to participate on equal terms.

## Why the Change Matters Scientifically

DOE describes a home as a system of interdependent parts, including site and climate, insulation and air sealing, windows, heating and cooling, appliances, lighting, and water heating. The current systems idea therefore fits residential building science, but its cases need to operate at household scale. [DOE: How to Design for Performance](https://bsesc.energy.gov/energy-basics/how-design-performance)

The air prototype needs particular care. EPA states that most home heating and cooling systems do not mechanically bring outdoor air indoors; outdoor air may instead enter through openings or a separate ventilation path. A school-style supply/return diagram would give students the wrong default picture of a home. [EPA: Improving Indoor Air Quality](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/improving-indoor-air-quality)

Home systems also interact when one feature changes. DOE links heat flow, air movement, and moisture at the home's enclosure, while EPA notes that air tightening without appropriate indoor-air and moisture considerations can create unintended effects. These sources support studying interactions and tradeoffs with supplied cases; they do not supply ready-made middle-school lesson data or justify a real-home diagnosis. [DOE: Heat Flow at the Home Envelope](https://bsesc.energy.gov/energy-basics/building-envelope-building-science-intro-heat-flow); [EPA: Energy, Weatherization and Indoor Air Quality](https://www.epa.gov/indoor-air-quality-iaq/energy-weatherization-and-indoor-air-quality)

## Developer Review Sequence

1. Review the proposed purpose, central idea, and learning-experience wording. Approve or revise the residential scope before it replaces language in the course concept or project brief.
2. Decide which candidate topics deserve space in the nine-week course. The audit does not assume that every existing prototype will become a unit.
3. Recast the retained examples around one or more ordinary fictional homes, keeping each case's evidence, model limits, reading level, and 40-minute pacing under review.
4. Revisit the candidate outcomes and culminating assessment with the residential cases before drafting the 27-meeting map.

All current prototypes remain development drafts. This packet makes their residential fit reviewable; it does not authorize classroom use, a final course map, or a particular home design.
