# Heat-Flow Thread Proposal

_Phase 1 design direction: approved for continued development; not yet a complete unit, lesson plan, or final course map._

## Purpose

Students need a durable way to explain why building spaces gain, lose, and redistribute thermal energy. This proposal develops heat flow as a recurring explanatory idea that can support the course's central idea that buildings are systems.

It is deliberately **not** a decision to make heat flow the sole course organizer or to reduce the topic to a self-contained three-session mini-unit. The final course map will determine when and how often students return to it.

## Candidate Driving Question

> How does thermal energy move through and around a building, and how can design choices change comfort and energy use?

## Candidate Student Understandings

By the end of the course, students may be able to:

- Explain that thermal energy moves from warmer to cooler places.
- Identify conduction, convection, and radiation in familiar building situations.
- Use a simple model to trace how heat moves through a building component or space.
- Use evidence to explain how a thermal boundary, shade, windows, insulation, or air movement can change heat flow.
- Recognize a relevant tradeoff or limitation, such as the relationship among shade, solar heat gain, daylight, ventilation, cost, and comfort.

These are candidate outcomes for review alongside the other course-level outcomes. They do not require professional heat-loss calculations, code interpretation, building diagnosis, or repair recommendations.

## Recurring Heat-Flow Explanation Routine

When a lesson involves thermal comfort or a related building-system question, students can use this five-step routine:

1. **Locate the temperature difference.** Identify the relatively warmer and cooler places or surfaces.
2. **Trace the path.** Draw arrows that show the plausible heat path.
3. **Name the mechanism.** Label conduction, convection, radiation, or a combination when supported by the evidence.
4. **Use evidence.** Connect the explanation to an observation, diagram, data display, model output, or source.
5. **Consider a response and tradeoff.** Describe a possible design choice and one limitation or question that remains.

The routine gives later lessons a common language without forcing every phenomenon into a heat-flow explanation.

## Candidate Learning Arc

The course map should distribute these encounters rather than lock them into a standalone unit:

| Encounter | Student work | Role in the thread |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Notice | Compare fictional room or building-envelope scenarios and make an initial model of where heat moves. | Elicit prior ideas and introduce temperature differences. |
| Explain | Use visual or data-based evidence to distinguish conduction, convection, and radiation. | Build accurate explanatory language. |
| Apply | Compare proposed design choices such as exterior shade, insulation, window design, or air movement. | Connect heat flow to comfort, energy, and tradeoffs. |
| Revisit | Use the routine in later air, moisture, sunlight, climate, or design lessons when it is relevant. | Show that a building system's parts interact. |

## Classroom and Safety Boundaries

- Design for a 40-minute standard classroom period and ordinary classroom technology.
- Prefer visual evidence, shared data, paper models, and low-cost demonstrations that have a defined learning purpose.
- Do not require a workshop, specialized construction tools, thermal cameras, or professional instrumentation.
- Use fictional or teacher-approved examples; students do not diagnose the actual school or make repair recommendations.
- Treat students' bodily comfort reports carefully and do not collect health or symptom information.

## Evidence of Learning

Appropriate student products could include an annotated heat-flow model, a short claim-evidence-tradeoff explanation, or a comparison of two design choices. These are possible assessment elements, not yet a final assessment plan.

## Evidence Basis

The U.S. Department of Energy describes building heat flow through conduction, convection, and radiation, and relates thermal boundaries to building comfort and energy efficiency: [Heat Flow — Building Science Education](https://bsesc.energy.gov/energy-basics/heat-flow).
