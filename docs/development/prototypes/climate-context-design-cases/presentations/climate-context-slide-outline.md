# Climate-Context Design Cases: Slide Outline

_Draft presentation content. Build an editable slide deck only if it improves delivery for the teacher._

Use simple diagrams, large text, and descriptive alt text. Do not use color as the only way to distinguish contexts, criteria, or options.

## Lesson 1: Read a Climate Context

| Slide | Purpose | Suggested content |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Title | Launch the sequence. | “How can climate context change building questions?” Include the driving question. |
| 2. Weather and climate | Establish the distinction. | One sentence for each term plus a simple timeline: today / many years. |
| 3. Fictional climate cards | Introduce the evidence. | Text-first Pine Crossing and Sun Mesa cards. State that they are invented instructional examples. |
| 4. Your job | Clarify the product. | “Use evidence to ask a system question. Do not diagnose a building.” Show the four-step reasoning routine. |

## Lesson 2: Compare a Design in Two Contexts

| Slide | Purpose | Suggested content |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5. Revisit | Connect to Lesson 1. | Ask: “What climate clue might matter for a window-area decision?” |
| 6. Design brief | Frame the fictional comparison. | Same reading room, two fictional contexts, three-credit limit, and four criteria. |
| 7. Model outputs | Make the data readable. | Recreate the context-by-option table. Add: “Fictional model outputs, not a construction prediction.” |
| 8. Make and qualify a claim | Support writing and discussion. | Sentence frames for two context-based claims, a tradeoff, and a limitation. |
| 9. Reusable routine | Reinforce the thread. | Read climate evidence; name a system question; compare outputs; make a context-based claim; qualify it. |

## Accessibility Check Before Use

- Climate facts appear as text, not only as a colored map.
- Data values and whether higher/lower is better are stated in words.
- Definitions are visible before students begin analysis.
- Slides use large, readable fonts and sufficient contrast.
