# Climate-Context Design Cases: Unit Overview

_Draft prototype — two 40-minute meetings._

## Purpose

Students compare two fictional climate contexts and use supplied model outputs to explain why a design choice can have different results in different places. The sequence connects weather, heat flow, sunlight, moisture, and design tradeoffs without requiring climate-code knowledge or local building analysis.

## Candidate Driving Question

> How can climate context change which building-design questions matter most?

## Candidate Learning Outcomes

Students will be able to:

- identify temperature and precipitation patterns in a supplied climate card;
- explain how a climate pattern could affect a building-system question;
- compare two fictional design options in two supplied contexts;
- use model outputs to make and qualify a context-based claim; and
- name a tradeoff or additional evidence needed.

## Sequence at a Glance

| Meeting | Question | Main student product |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Read | What climate evidence matters in this fictional place? | Climate-context evidence map |
| 2. Compare | How do the same design options perform differently in two fictional contexts? | Context-based claim-evidence-tradeoff explanation |

## Shared Reasoning Routine

1. Read the supplied climate evidence.
2. Name a building-system question the pattern could raise.
3. Compare the supplied model outputs; do not assume one choice is always best.
4. Make a claim tied to the stated criteria and context.
5. Name a tradeoff, limitation, or additional information needed.

## Materials

- Teacher display and student laptops, or printed copies
- Pencils, highlighters, or digital annotation tools
- Optional chart paper or shared digital board

No sensors, field trips, construction tools, weather stations, or measurements of the actual school are required.

## Vocabulary

- **climate:** patterns in temperature, precipitation, and other weather conditions over many years
- **context:** the conditions that help explain a situation
- **precipitation:** water that falls from clouds, such as rain or snow
- **design criterion:** a feature a solution should achieve
- **constraint:** a limit a solution must stay within
- **tradeoff:** a benefit gained while accepting a cost, limitation, or reduced benefit elsewhere
- **model output:** a simplified result supplied for learning; it is not a real construction prediction

## Science Background for the Teacher

DOE describes climate zones using factors including heating degree days, average temperatures, and precipitation. In real building science, many details affect a design decision. This prototype intentionally reduces that complexity: students use broad fictional climate patterns and supplied model outputs to learn that context matters. Do not turn the comparison into a code, product, or repair recommendation. [DOE: Climate Zones](https://www.energy.gov/cmei/buildings/climate-zones)

## Assessment Evidence

Use the [Climate-Context Reasoning Rubric](assessments/climate-context-reasoning-rubric.md) to review the evidence map and final explanation. Prioritize justified reasoning and appropriate uncertainty over a single “best” design answer.

## Accessibility and Participation

- Provide climate cards in text and display forms; do not rely on map colors alone.
- Define each vocabulary word before analysis begins and offer a visible word bank.
- Allow students to draw, type, dictate, or explain their reasoning aloud.
- Let partners take roles such as climate-evidence reader, data checker, claim writer, and questioner.
- Offer a partially completed evidence map when needed.

## Prototype Review Questions

- Do students distinguish long-term climate patterns from a single day's weather?
- Can students avoid treating a fictional data table as a universal design rule?
- Does the routine connect naturally to heat-flow and moisture reasoning without overloading the course?
- Is this a useful basis for deciding whether climate should be an essential or supporting course strand?
