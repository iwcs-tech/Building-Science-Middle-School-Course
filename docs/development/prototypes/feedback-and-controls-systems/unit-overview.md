# Feedback-and-Controls Systems: Unit Overview

_Draft prototype — two 40-minute meetings._

## Purpose

Students use supplied fictional evidence to model a feedback loop in a bounded learning space. They connect a changing condition to a monitored signal, a decision rule, and a modeled response, then compare two fictional control plans without treating either as a recommendation for a real building.

## Candidate Driving Question

> How can a building system use information about changing conditions, and what tradeoffs can follow from different response rules?

## Candidate Learning Outcomes

Students will be able to:

- identify a changing condition, monitored signal, decision rule, response, and feedback in a supplied fictional system;
- use arrows and supplied evidence to make a qualified feedback-loop model;
- explain why a model output does not establish what a real building should do;
- compare fictional control plans using stated criteria and constraints; and
- make a claim that includes evidence, a tradeoff, and a limitation or question.

## Sequence at a Glance

| Meeting | Question | Main student product |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Model | What information moves through this fictional feedback loop? | Labeled loop model with evidence notes and an uncertainty |
| 2. Compare | Which fictional response rule best fits the stated priorities, and what does its model leave out? | Claim-evidence-tradeoff-limitation explanation |

## Shared Reasoning Routine

1. Bound the fictional system.
2. Mark the condition that changes.
3. Trace the signal, decision rule, and modeled response.
4. Add a feedback arrow showing what the model checks next.
5. Cite evidence for each arrow and name an uncertainty.
6. Compare stated choices using criteria, constraints, tradeoffs, and limitations.

## Materials

- Teacher display and student laptops, or printed student pages
- Pencils, highlighters, or digital annotation tools
- Optional chart paper or shared digital board

No sensors, field trip, equipment access, measurements of the actual school, or operation of any building system are required.

## Vocabulary

- **condition:** something in a system that can change
- **signal:** information sent from a monitor in a model
- **decision rule:** an if-then instruction used in a model
- **response:** the modeled action that follows a rule
- **feedback:** information about the result that returns to affect the next response
- **criterion:** a feature a choice should achieve
- **constraint:** a limit a choice must stay within
- **tradeoff:** a benefit gained while accepting a cost, limitation, or reduced benefit elsewhere

## Science Background for the Teacher

Real building controls involve equipment, people, design intent, maintenance, and conditions that are far more complex than this instructional model. EPA identifies documented operational checks of sensors and controls as part of professional building commissioning. Students here reason only from the supplied fictional dashboard and response cards. The simplified loop helps make a systems interaction visible; it does not evaluate a real system or establish an appropriate setting.

- [EPA: Building Commissioning for Schools](https://www.epa.gov/iaq-schools/building-commissioning-part-indoor-air-quality-design-tools-schools)

## Assessment Evidence

Use the [Feedback-and-Controls Reasoning Rubric](assessments/feedback-and-controls-rubric.md) to review the Lesson 1 model and Lesson 2 explanation. Prioritize accurate system relationships, evidence, and appropriate limits over selecting a single “best” plan.

## Accessibility and Participation

- Provide the dashboard in both table and text-and-arrow formats; do not rely on color alone.
- Preview vocabulary with a visible word bank and optional icons.
- Let students draw, type, dictate, or explain their model aloud.
- Assign partner roles such as system-boundary reader, evidence finder, arrow checker, and questioner.
- Offer a partly completed loop or comparison table when needed.

## Prototype Review Questions

- Can students distinguish a signal from the condition it represents?
- Do students understand feedback as information used in a next cycle rather than proof that a system is “fixed”?
- Does the fictional comparison make criteria, constraints, tradeoffs, and uncertainty visible within 40 minutes?
- Does the boundary against real-system operation and diagnosis remain clear?
