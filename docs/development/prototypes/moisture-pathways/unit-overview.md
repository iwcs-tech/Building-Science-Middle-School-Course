# Moisture Pathways: Unit Overview

_Draft prototype — two 40-minute meetings._

## Purpose

Students learn to model a moisture situation as a source, a path, and an effect. They use supplied evidence to distinguish liquid water, water carried with moving air, and condensation. The sequence makes visible a building-system idea that can later connect with heat flow, air movement, materials, durability, and comfort.

## Candidate Driving Question

> How can water enter, move through, or collect in a building—and why does its path matter?

## Candidate Learning Outcomes

Students will be able to:

- distinguish observations from inferences in a fictional building case;
- create a source → path → effect model using supplied evidence;
- identify liquid-water movement, water carried with moving air, or condensation in simplified examples;
- explain why a colder surface can matter when condensation occurs; and
- make a claim using evidence while naming one limitation or additional question.

## Sequence at a Glance

| Meeting | Question | Main student product |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Trace | What source, path, and effect can we model from this evidence? | Annotated source → path → effect model |
| 2. Defend | Which moisture pathway best explains this fictional case, and what information is missing? | Claim–evidence–limitation explanation |

## Shared Explanation Routine

1. Identify what is observed.
2. Locate a plausible water source.
3. Trace a possible path.
4. Describe an effect on a surface or material.
5. State what evidence supports the model and what information is still needed.

## Materials

- Teacher display and student laptops, or printed student pages
- Pencils, highlighters, or digital annotation tools
- Optional scissors for the Lesson 2 case cards
- Optional chart paper or shared digital board

No specialized sensors, moisture meters, workshop tools, samples from the school, or off-campus experiences are required.

## Vocabulary

- **moisture:** water in liquid form or as water vapor in air
- **source:** where the water or water vapor begins in a model
- **path:** the route water or moisture may take
- **effect:** a change observed after moisture reaches a place or material
- **condensation:** water vapor changing into liquid water on a surface
- **water vapor:** water in gas form, mixed with air
- **evidence:** observations or information that supports a claim
- **limitation:** a fact the model does not establish or a question it cannot answer yet

## Science Background for the Teacher

DOE building-science guidance explains that moisture can move as bulk liquid water, by capillary action, with air flow, and by vapor diffusion. It also explains that temperature, climate, building materials, and construction details affect how moisture behaves. For middle school students, this prototype uses three intentionally simplified pathways: liquid water, water carried with moving air, and condensation. Do not imply that these three labels fully diagnose a real building or that condensation alone proves a cause. [DOE: Intro Moisture Flow](https://bsesc.energy.gov/energy-basics/building-enclosure-building-science-intro-moisture-flow)

## Assessment Evidence

Use the [Moisture Pathways Rubric](assessments/moisture-pathways-rubric.md) to review the Lesson 1 model and Lesson 2 explanation. Prioritize evidence-based reasoning and appropriate uncertainty over one predetermined answer.

## Accessibility and Participation

- Provide every case in text and display form; do not rely on color alone.
- Allow students to draw, type, dictate, or explain their model aloud.
- Preteach vocabulary with the slide outline and a visible word bank.
- Let groups assign roles such as reader, evidence finder, path tracer, and reporter.
- Offer a partially completed source → path → effect diagram when needed.

## Prototype Review Questions

- Can students distinguish an observation from an inference?
- Do the three pathway categories clarify or oversimplify student thinking?
- Does the limitation prompt keep students from overclaiming?
- Is the sequence a useful complement to the heat-flow prototype?
