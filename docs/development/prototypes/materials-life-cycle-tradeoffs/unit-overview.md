# Materials Life-Cycle Tradeoffs: Unit Overview

_Draft prototype — two 40-minute meetings._

## Purpose

Students learn that a material's impacts and choices can involve more than the moment it is installed. They trace simplified stages—resource acquisition, manufacture, use/maintenance, and end-of-use management—then compare two fictional interior-panel options using stated criteria and constraints.

The sequence connects materials, durability, resource use, cost, and design tradeoffs without teaching real product selection, procurement, or construction practice.

## Candidate Driving Question

> What questions should we ask before calling a material choice “better” for a building?

## Candidate Learning Outcomes

Students will be able to:

- sequence four simplified material life-cycle stages;
- explain that a choice can have effects at more than one stage;
- compare supplied fictional material-option data against stated criteria and a constraint;
- make an evidence-based choice while naming a tradeoff; and
- state a limitation or additional information needed.

## Sequence at a Glance

| Meeting | Question | Main student product |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Trace | What happens to a material before, during, and after its use in a building? | Material journey map |
| 2. Compare | Which fictional option best fits the stated goals, and what tradeoff remains? | Claim-evidence-tradeoff-limitation explanation |

## Shared Reasoning Routine

1. Name the material-life-cycle stage.
2. Identify a possible effect or question at that stage.
3. Compare supplied evidence across more than one stage.
4. Make a claim based on the stated criteria and constraint.
5. Name a tradeoff, limitation, or additional evidence needed.

## Materials

- Teacher display and student laptops, or printed copies
- Pencils, highlighters, or digital annotation tools
- Optional scissors for cutting and sequencing the journey cards
- Optional chart paper or shared digital board

No material samples, tools, product research, field trips, or observations of the actual school are required.

## Vocabulary

- **material:** matter used to make an object or building part
- **life cycle:** stages a material or product moves through from resources to end-of-use management
- **resource acquisition:** obtaining the raw materials used to make something
- **manufacture:** turning raw materials into a usable product
- **use and maintenance:** the time a product is used and cared for
- **end-of-use management:** what happens when a product is removed, reused, recycled, or discarded
- **criterion:** a feature a solution should achieve
- **constraint:** a limit a solution must stay within
- **tradeoff:** a benefit gained while accepting a cost, limitation, or reduced benefit elsewhere

## Science Background for the Teacher

EPA describes material life cycles as including raw-material acquisition, manufacture, production, use/reuse/maintenance, and waste management. It explains that looking across stages can reveal opportunities to reduce impacts, conserve resources, and reduce costs. This prototype uses four intentionally simplified stages and invented option data so students can practice systems reasoning without treating a single rating as a real environmental verdict. [EPA: Sustainable Materials Management Basics](https://www.epa.gov/smm/sustainable-materials-management-basics)

## Assessment Evidence

Use the [Materials Tradeoff Reasoning Rubric](assessments/materials-tradeoff-reasoning-rubric.md) to review the Lesson 1 journey map and Lesson 2 explanation. Prioritize evidence-based comparison and appropriate uncertainty over a single preferred option.

## Accessibility and Participation

- Provide every card and data table in text and display form; do not rely on colors or icons alone.
- Preteach vocabulary with the slide outline and a visible word bank.
- Allow students to draw, type, dictate, or orally explain their reasoning.
- Let groups assign roles such as stage sequencer, evidence finder, criteria checker, and reporter.
- Offer a partially completed journey map or reduced two-option matrix when needed.

## Prototype Review Questions

- Can students distinguish a life-cycle stage from a material's physical property?
- Do students avoid treating one fictional rating as proof that an option is universally best?
- Does the task make environmental impact and cost discussable without product research or procurement advice?
- Is the sequence a useful basis for deciding whether materials should be an essential or supporting course strand?
