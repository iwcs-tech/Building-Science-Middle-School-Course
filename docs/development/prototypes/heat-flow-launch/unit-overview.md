# Heat-Flow Launch: Unit Overview

_Draft prototype — three 40-minute meetings._

## Purpose

This launch helps students begin using a shared heat-flow explanation routine. It introduces thermal energy moving from warmer to cooler places and the three mechanisms of heat transfer: conduction, convection, and radiation. It prepares students to revisit heat flow when later lessons address sunlight, air movement, moisture, climate, insulation, or design tradeoffs.

The sequence is not intended to make heat flow the sole organizer of the course or to teach professional building analysis.

## Candidate Driving Question

> How does thermal energy move through and around a building, and how can design choices change comfort and energy use?

## Candidate Learning Outcomes

Students will be able to:

- identify relative temperature differences in a simple building scenario;
- model a plausible path for thermal energy using arrows and labels;
- distinguish conduction, convection, and radiation in simplified building examples;
- use a provided evidence table to compare design choices; and
- communicate a claim, evidence, tradeoff, and limitation.

## Sequence at a Glance

| Meeting | Question | Main student product |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Notice | Where might thermal energy be moving in this fictional classroom? | Initial annotated heat-flow model |
| 2. Explain | Which mechanism best explains each simplified example? | Revised model with mechanism labels and evidence |
| 3. Apply | Which design option best meets the stated criteria and constraints? | Claim-evidence-tradeoff explanation |

## Shared Explanation Routine

Students return to these steps whenever heat flow is relevant:

1. Locate the temperature difference.
2. Trace a plausible heat path.
3. Name the mechanism or mechanisms.
4. Use available evidence.
5. State a design response, tradeoff, or remaining limitation.

## Materials

- Teacher display and student laptops, or printed copies of the student pages
- Pencils, highlighters, or digital annotation tools
- Optional scissors for the Lesson 2 evidence-card sort
- Optional sticky notes or chart paper for sharing models

No specialized sensors, thermal cameras, workshop equipment, construction tools, or measurements of the actual school are required.

## Vocabulary

- **thermal energy:** energy associated with the motion of particles in matter
- **heat flow:** thermal energy moving because places or objects have different temperatures
- **conduction:** heat transfer through direct contact
- **convection:** heat transfer through moving air or another fluid
- **radiation:** energy transfer by waves that can travel through space
- **thermal boundary:** building parts, such as walls, roofs, windows, and doors, that separate conditioned indoor space from outdoors
- **tradeoff:** a benefit gained while accepting a cost, limitation, or reduced benefit elsewhere

## Science Background for the Teacher

The Department of Energy describes conduction, convection, and radiation as the three mechanisms of heat flow in buildings. Thermal boundaries—including insulation, windows, and doors—affect comfort and energy efficiency. In real buildings, several mechanisms often act at once; the lesson cards simplify examples so students can learn to identify a dominant mechanism before handling more complex systems. [DOE: Heat Flow](https://bsesc.energy.gov/energy-basics/heat-flow)

## Assessment Evidence

Use the [Heat-Flow Explanation Rubric](assessments/heat-flow-explanation-rubric.md) to review the initial model, revised model, and final explanation. The final product prioritizes sound reasoning over a single predetermined design answer.

## Accessibility and Participation

- Provide the scenario and data in both display and text form; do not rely on color alone.
- Allow students to draw, type, dictate, or verbally explain their models.
- Preteach vocabulary with the slide outline or a small word bank.
- Assign group roles during card sorting and design comparison so each student can contribute.
- Provide the fictional data in larger print or an accessible digital document when needed.

## Prototype Review Questions

- Can students complete the model and explanation in the available time?
- Which examples clarify the mechanisms, and which create misconceptions?
- Does the final design comparison elicit useful evidence-based discussion?
- What support is needed before students revisit heat flow in later course work?
