# Systems-Model Evidence Progression

_Developer-facing Phase 1 review packet. It proposes a common learning progression; it does not approve final course outcomes, content strands, assessment, or a course map._

## Purpose

This packet gives the developing course a way to connect existing and future building-science cases without making heat flow, moisture, or any other topic the sole organizer. It proposes a progression in which students identify a bounded building system, trace a supported interaction, revise a model with evidence, and compare a response with its tradeoffs and limits.

The progression is designed for the course's central idea: **buildings are systems whose parts interact with people and the environment.** It is a candidate basis for reviewing course-level outcomes before the nine-week map is drafted.

## Candidate Course-Level Outcomes

By the end of the course, students may be able to:

1. **Bound a system.** Identify relevant building parts, people, and environmental conditions in a supplied scenario.
2. **Trace an interaction.** Use arrows, labels, or a short explanation to show a plausible cause-and-effect relationship involving heat, air, moisture, light, sound, materials, weather, or people.
3. **Revise with evidence.** Distinguish an observation or supplied data point from an inference, then add, remove, or qualify part of a model.
4. **Compare a response.** Use stated criteria and constraints to defend a design choice while naming a tradeoff, limitation, or additional evidence needed.

These are candidate outcomes, not a final grading or standards-alignment decision. They intentionally ask students to reason from fictional or teacher-approved evidence rather than diagnose the actual school, interpret codes, or recommend repairs.

## Four-Stage Student Progression

| Stage | Student move | Useful product | Teacher look-for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Notice the system | Name relevant parts and conditions inside and outside a stated boundary. | Labeled scenario diagram or notice-and-wonder list | Students do not treat a building part in isolation. |
| 2. Make a first model | Draw or describe a plausible interaction and identify uncertainty. | Arrow model with a question mark or limitation | The claim fits the supplied scenario without overclaiming. |
| 3. Revise with evidence | Add a mechanism, data point, or new connection after comparing evidence. | Revised model with an evidence note | The revision changes or strengthens the reasoning, not just the appearance. |
| 4. Apply and qualify | Compare choices against criteria, constraints, tradeoffs, and limitations. | Claim-evidence-tradeoff-limitation explanation | Students make a defensible choice and recognize what the model cannot establish. |

## Current Prototype Crosswalk

| Candidate outcome | Heat-Flow Launch evidence | Moisture Pathways evidence | Development implication |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bound a system | Students identify warmer/cooler locations, thermal-boundary parts, and sunlight in fictional Classroom Alder. | Students identify a source, route, affected surface or material, and temperature condition. | Both drafts already support an explicit system boundary prompt. |
| Trace an interaction | Students trace heat paths and label conduction, convection, or radiation. | Students trace source → path → effect and distinguish three simplified pathways. | The language can remain topic-specific while the model structure stays consistent. |
| Revise with evidence | Students revise an initial model after mechanism evidence cards. | Students defend a pathway using supplied case evidence and identify missing information. | A shared annotation key could make revision visible across units. |
| Compare a response | Students compare fictional shade options using modeled outputs, criteria, and a budget constraint. | Students name a limitation rather than prescribe a repair. | A later climate or materials case can extend design reasoning without selecting a final capstone. |

## Review-Only Trial Prompt

Use this short prompt only to help an educator judge the proposed progression. It is not a required lesson or a diagnosis task.

> **Fictional scenario:** During a cool, rainy week, a library's entry mat is damp at the end of the school day. A nearby exterior door opens often. A window above the mat has no direct rain on it. The space is warmer than outdoors.
>
> **Supplied evidence:** a simple floor plan, a weather card that says “rainy and cool,” a statement that the door opened 60 times, and a photo-like fictional diagram showing wet footprints near the entrance.
>
> **Student task:** Draw a system boundary around the parts that matter. Trace one plausible interaction. Circle one item that is an observation and write one question that would help improve the model. Do not recommend a fix.

### Quick Review Criteria

- Can students name a boundary without assuming they have identified the real cause?
- Does the task make evidence and uncertainty visible?
- Is the vocabulary accessible without adding a separate content strand?
- Could this routine recur within a 40-minute period without displacing needed content learning?

## Suggested Developer Review Decision

After reviewing this packet alongside the existing prototypes, decide whether the four outcomes should be retained, revised, reduced, or replaced before Phase 2 course mapping. That decision remains with the course developer and educator reviewers.

## Evidence Basis

The National Academies describes systems and system models, cause and effect, and scientific practices as connected dimensions of science learning. NGSS middle-school engineering-design guidance expects students to develop, use, and revise models; evaluate solutions using criteria and constraints; and use evidence in arguments.

- [A Framework for K-12 Science Education — National Academies](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/resource/13165/interactive/)
- [Middle School Engineering Design — NGSS](https://www.nextgenscience.org/sites/ngss/files/MS%20ETS%20topics%20combined%206.12.13.pdf)
