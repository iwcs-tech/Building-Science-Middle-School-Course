# Middle School Building Science Course Concept

_A working concept for a nine-week, open-source building science course for seventh- and eighth-grade students._

## Purpose

This course introduces students to the idea that a building is not merely a box with four walls. A building is a dynamic system in which heat, air, moisture, light, sound, materials, weather, and people continually interact.

The course should help students notice the science occurring in ordinary buildings, ask useful questions about what they observe, and use evidence to develop simple explanations.

This document describes the course itself. The process used to research, design, test, and publish the course is described in [Course Development Process](course-development-process.md).

## Initial Course Setting

- **Grade levels:** Seventh and eighth grade
- **Typical enrollment:** Approximately 15 students, with flexibility for changing enrollment
- **Course length:** Nine weeks
- **Meeting schedule:** Three days per week
- **Class period:** 40 minutes
- **Total meetings:** 27
- **Total instructional time:** 18 hours
- **Setting:** Standard classroom without a workshop or dedicated construction tools
- **Classroom technology:** Teacher laptop, 75-inch BenQ presentation board, and one laptop per student
- **Field experiences:** At most one field trip should be assumed

## Central Course Idea

> Buildings are systems. Their parts interact with one another, with the people inside them, and with the environment around them.

The course should make this idea visible through familiar questions and observable phenomena rather than through professional-level technical training.

## Preliminary Learning Direction

The final learning outcomes still need to be developed. As a starting point, students may learn to:

- Recognize scientific processes occurring in and around buildings.
- Explain simple cause-and-effect relationships involving heat, air, moisture, light, sound, materials, weather, and people.
- Observe a building carefully and ask productive scientific questions about it.
- Gather and interpret basic observations or measurements.
- Use evidence to support an explanation or recommendation.
- Recognize that building decisions involve tradeoffs among comfort, health, durability, energy, environmental impact, and cost.

These statements are provisional and should be refined before the full course is outlined.

## Possible Content Strands

The course may include some combination of the following:

- Buildings as interconnected systems
- Heat transfer and thermal comfort
- Air movement and ventilation
- Water, moisture, and condensation
- Light, shade, and solar energy
- Sound and acoustics
- Materials and their properties
- Energy use and efficiency
- Indoor environmental quality and human health
- The relationship between buildings and local climate

The course should not attempt to cover every strand equally. Topics should be selected and organized around a small number of engaging questions appropriate for a nine-week course.

### Developing Heat Flow as a Recurring Idea

Heat transfer and thermal comfort will be developed as a recurring explanatory idea rather than as a self-contained mini-unit. Students should use heat-flow reasoning when it helps explain familiar building phenomena and design tradeoffs. This does not make heat flow the course's sole organizing concept or settle the final set, sequence, or relative emphasis of the remaining content strands. The current Phase 1 design proposal is maintained in the [Heat-Flow Thread Proposal](docs/development/heat-flow-thread-proposal.md).

## Learning Experience

The first version should be designed for teacher-led classroom instruction. Lessons may combine:

- Short teacher explanations and visual presentations
- Discussion of familiar building experiences
- Brief observations of the classroom or school building
- Laptop-based research, analysis, simulations, or documentation
- Simple, inexpensive investigations that can be completed safely in a standard classroom
- Collaborative problem-solving and communication
- A culminating task that helps students explain how a building works as a system

Hands-on investigations and observations should be used when they support a learning goal. The course should not assume access to a workshop, specialized tools, construction materials, or repeated off-campus visits.

## Possible Course Formats

Development should begin with the teacher-led classroom course. After it has been tested, it may be adapted into:

- A fully online, self-paced course
- Flexible open-source materials that educators can revise for their students and local context
- Other versions that combine classroom instruction with online or AI-supported resources

AI tools used to develop the curriculum are not automatically part of the student or teacher experience. Any student- or teacher-facing use of AI should be considered separately and included only when it advances a clear learning goal.

## Questions Still to Resolve

- What should students know, understand, and be able to do by the end of the nine weeks?
- What central questions or phenomena should organize the course?
- Which content strands are essential, and which should be omitted or treated as optional?
- What culminating task would best demonstrate the central idea that a building is a system?
- What kinds of investigations are realistic within a 40-minute class period?
- How should student learning be assessed?
- What field trip, if any, would add enough value to justify the logistical demands?
- How should the classroom course later be adapted for self-paced online learning?
